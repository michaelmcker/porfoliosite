The problem: turning local demand into useful enquiries

Cool Runnings needed a web presence that could explain its landscaping services and help local customers get in touch. The opportunity was broader than making a homepage look finished: service information, local search and the request process had to work together.

For a landscaping business, a useful enquiry includes the kind of work, the property location and enough context to arrange the next conversation. A visitor comparing companies needs to understand whether the business covers the work before asking for an estimate. Those decisions shaped the site and the content.

The original Cool Runnings case study shows the website and local-search system behind the project.

1. Give the services enough detail to be found and understood

Cool Runnings’ website presents the services as concrete kinds of work customers can recognise. Service content gives local searches somewhere useful to land and helps the visitor understand what the business can do.

That structure also makes the enquiry more specific. A customer reading about yard maintenance arrives with a different need from someone considering sod installation or rock work. The page should support that distinction instead of sending every visitor through a generic introduction.

The practical lesson for another trade is to start with its actual service list. Find the questions that affect the first conversation and put those answers on the relevant page.

2. Add local information with a purpose

The Cool Runnings content system brings together city, service, neighbourhood, weather, frost and growing-season information. The point is to make local pages useful for the work, rather than adding town names to identical copy.

For a landscaping customer, timing and property conditions can affect planning. Local context belongs on a page when it helps explain the service, prepare for a conversation or understand the next step. The page structure, internal links, canonical URLs and sitemap organise that information into a coherent website.

Another industry needs different local detail. An accommodation page needs location and arrival information; a professional practice needs its actual office and appointment process. The content model should follow the business.

3. Give visitors something useful before they enquire

Cool Runnings’ website includes practical guides and calculators alongside the service content. These resources help visitors work through questions rather than leaving them with a service name and a contact button.

A useful tool belongs close to the service it supports. Explain what the output means and which details still need a conversation or site assessment. The website can help a visitor prepare without pretending that a simple calculation is a final professional quote.

For another business, the right resource may be a preparation checklist, a clear scope comparison or an explanation of the booking process. Choose the resource from customer questions, not from a requirement to add something interactive.

4. Make the next step direct and understandable

Cool Runnings’ conversion work connects the service information to calls and estimate enquiries. A clear next step gives someone who has understood the offer a way to move forward.

For an Okanagan service business, the handoff matters as much as the button. The request needs to reach the right person with the location and work described clearly enough to respond. Website copy, form design and the response process should agree about what happens next.

The same review can expose friction elsewhere: a contact link that is difficult to find on a phone, a form that asks too much or a confirmation that implies a booking before one is agreed.

The result: 30% more qualified bookings

Cool Runnings saw a 30% increase in qualified bookings following the website, local SEO and conversion work. Qualified bookings are the business outcome described here. The percentage should not be relabelled as revenue growth, a traffic increase or a lift caused by one isolated design change.

Search visibility and website actions help explain how a site is being used, but they are different measurements. A click can lead to a conversation; it does not establish that a job was booked. Keeping those stages separate makes the reporting useful for business decisions.

The project shows the value of connecting discovery, useful content and a practical enquiry path. It also provides a stronger basis for a consultation than a promise that a new design alone will create more work.

What an Okanagan business can apply

A service business can use the Cool Runnings approach by starting with the work it wants to win and building the website around that customer’s questions.

  • Choose the priority services and the area you actually cover.
  • Write useful answers for each service and meaningful local differences.
  • Show relevant work and make the request route easy to complete.
  • Check who receives the enquiry and how the next conversation happens.
  • Track qualified bookings separately from traffic and button taps.

Questions, answered.

Does the 30% result refer to revenue?

No. The Cool Runnings result refers to an increase in jobs and qualified bookings. It should not be presented as a revenue or website traffic increase.

Do I need calculators and a large website to use this approach?

No. Start with useful service information, relevant evidence and a working enquiry route. Add tools or more content when they solve a real customer question and the business can maintain them.

Can the same approach work outside landscaping?

The principles can apply to other service businesses, but the content should change. A clinic needs appointment and practitioner information; an accountant needs scope and preparation details; a trade needs service coverage and a practical estimate request.
